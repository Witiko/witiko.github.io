Two days after [returning from _TUG 2026_ in Canada][1], I participated in my
first-ever LARP (Live Action Role Play). It was the sixth run of [the _97 Poets
of Revachol_][2], an international production for about a hundred players, held
over five days in a former 19th-century military hospital in Terezín, Czechia,
and set in the world of the award-winning _Disco Elysium (2019)_ video game.

In this post, I discuss the experience and what I learnt about both LARPs and
myself.

<!-- more -->

# Why I went
Most LARP players my age who I know, including my wife, have been LARPing
since high school, and I get the impression that it is somewhat unusual to
start at the age of 33. Not that there is anything wrong with that per se, but
most people who enjoy LARP seem to discover it much earlier in life.

What's more, I have had difficulty forming social bonds and navigating social
situations from a young age and never quite learned to feel at ease socially,
eventually settling into the rather solitary work of a tech consultant and
contractor. It may therefore seem like a big step to make my first LARP an
international event lasting five days and involving dozens of people.

My motivation was a combination of several things: I was excited to try
something new that my wife and many people in her social circle enjoyed, I had
thoroughly enjoyed _Disco Elysium_, and, despite my social unease, the sheer
scope of the LARP was exciting to me in a way that a smaller event might not
have been. I willed myself through the registration process before I could
change my mind, thinking, "What's the worst that could possibly happen?"

# How it went before the LARP
In the year leading up to the LARP, new materials were gradually released.
Profiles of all the players' characters, including pictures and short
biographies, were available online fairly early, along with general
descriptions of the different factions they belonged to. Information about the
setting and the broad shape of the story was also shared before the game.

At the time, this seemed strange to me. I felt that it might spoil some of the
fun of discovering these things during play, and I was unsure how much I was
expected to prepare based on the information provided. This feeling became even
stronger when, during the workshops immediately before the LARP, I received a
schedule for the coming days listing various events in advance.

My confusion stemmed from taking video games as the closest analogy. As it
turns out, however, a content LARP like the _Poets_ is more like a stage play:
the script is out in the open; the fun lies in bringing it to life.

Players still discover plenty of new information through their interactions
with other characters, so the "script" seems to be there mostly to (a) set
things in motion, allowing the small society of players to sustain itself for
several days without the organizers having to act as _deus ex machina_ and
constantly intervene to keep the game on track, and (b) give more ambitious
players the opportunity to prepare intricate props and scenarios in advance
rather than improvise everything on the spot.

In April, a costume guide arrived. Unlike at some other LARPs, where players
are expected to provide their own costumes, here the organizers would provide
most layers of clothing except for a few items such as shoes, underwear, and a
skirt or trousers. Based on my understanding of _Disco_'s 70s/80s aesthetic
and of my character, I spent a day shopping for clothes, then threw them into a
corner of the room and promptly forgot all about it.

In June, one of the players who was playing my character's child contacted me
via Discord, and we discussed the parent-child relationship, including how the
characters might interact, argue, and respond to different kinds of behavior.

## July 22: From Brno to Terezín and evening workshops
After [returning from Canada][1] and spending a day in Brno unsuccessfully
trying to recover from jet lag, I travelled to Prague on July 22 and took the
player bus to Terezín. The other players came from all over the world and
seemed extremely social. After arriving in Terezín around 7 PM, some of us,
myself included, checked into a nearby hotel, while the others were going to
sleep at the game location.

After checking in and arriving at the game location, players had a couple of
hours to register at a tent, pick up their characters' items from their
apartments and the costume room, eat dinner, dress as their characters, and
then head to the workshops scheduled for the evening. My mistake was deciding
to take my character's items back to the hotel, thinking I would make it back
in time for dinner. Instead, I got lost in Terezín and arrived late, so Kris,
one of the organizers, brought me dinner while I took part in the first
workshop. Not a great start.

The workshops began with an introduction to the game world and the basic game
mechanics. We learned, for example, how to signal that we were speaking
"off-game" as ourselves rather than as our characters, how to move through a
scene without being acknowledged when our characters had no reason to be
there, and how to call an immediate "red stop" if someone was injured, a
personal boundary had been crossed, or the scene otherwise needed to end
immediately.

These introductory workshops for all players were followed by a guided tour of
the entire game location, which occupied rooms across two floors of the old
hospital building as well as several underground spaces. After that came
meetings in smaller groups, such as the characters' families and other core
groups, and finally a speed-dating-style session with other important
characters my character already knew. In both cases, the goal was to get to
know one another and establish some shared ground rules for our interactions.

## July 23: The second day of workshops, and the beginning of the game
The second day began with another round of workshops, this time covering the
mechanics and game systems for weapons, shops, stealing, work, in-game mail,
creating art, drugs, intimacy, dancing, group violence, age, money, the [unseen
voices][3], and more.

Afterwards, we met with our secondary groups, such as our factions, before
moving to our characters' starting locations. The game itself began at 4 PM.

# How it went during the LARP
Rather than describing (and spoiling) the timeline of in-game events, let me
now focus on my personal thoughts and experiences, both in-game and off-game.

## Roommates
I shared the hotel room with three other people. One of them was a high-school
teacher from Sweden whom we spoke with quite a lot. He gave me tips on other
interesting international LARPs, such as [_Eclipse_][4], [_Daemon_][5], [_the Last
Call_][10], and others, as well as on recent video games similar to _Disco
Elysium_, such as _Esoteric Ebb (2026)_.

In exchange, I recommended some other interesting video games and a few places
they should visit in Prague after the LARP ended.

## The missing skills
It became clear almost immediately that LARPing draws on a peculiar mix of
social confidence, improvisation, acting ability, and a good memory for people:
all muscles I have exercised rather little and sometimes wonder whether I
possess at all. I found the combination demanding from the start.

I was expected to approach strangers and hold conversations as a character with
goals, opinions, relationships, and a history of their own; keep track of
roughly a hundred faces and names; and, more generally, function as a member of
this small fictional society. I found that quite overwhelming.

As a result, I spent a fair amount of time in the off-game room collecting
myself and talking things through with the organizers. Special thanks go to
Kai, who was particularly helpful, and to the organizers in the prop room, who
patiently answered far more of my questions than they probably expected to
during the game.

**UPDATE (2026-09-04):** I find that, in journalling, the things that most
readily lend themselves to writing are often those that I am already aware of
and comfortable writing about. As a result, they may also be the more banal and
obvious parts of the self-reflection that perhaps don't deserve as much
attention, since they are things I am already consciously or subconsciously
trying to correct for. By the same token, I am not necessarily convinced that
social and acting skills were the only or even main things I was missing.

Some of the major themes of the LARP were (_Disco Elysium_'s take on)
20th-century history, immigration, political extremism and violence, as well
as other topics such as poverty and drug abuse. I am privileged enough to be
partly ignorant about many of these subjects, both in terms of the relevant
real-world and fictional history and in terms of my limited engagement with
contemporary social and political issues. This made parts of the world and
some of the characters more difficult for me to understand and engage with.
One advantage of attending the LARP, then, was that it made those gaps in my
knowledge and understanding much clearer to me.

## Having fun
During the LARP, there was an unusual emphasis on having fun. The organizers
repeatedly stressed that the most important thing was to have fun and to make
sure that others had the opportunity to have fun as well. Whenever I went to
the off-game room, one of the first questions from the organizers or other
players was whether I was having fun.

On the one hand, this is understandable. The organizers had spent countless
hours of unpaid preparation trying to make the LARP work and be enjoyable, and
the players had taken time off and often travelled halfway across the world to
take part. More generally, the LARP community seems to attract, or perhaps
produce, remarkably kind and empathetic people. On the other hand, it is not
always entirely clear whether someone is having fun.

Early on, some of the players from my core group introduced me to the idea of
different types of fun:

- Type 1 fun is fun both in the moment and in retrospect.
- Type 2 fun is stressful or unpleasant in the moment but becomes satisfying
  or enjoyable when you look back on it later.
- Type 3 fun is simply not fun at all.

It was unclear to me then, and it is still not entirely clear almost two
months later, whether I spent most of the LARP having type 2 or type 3 fun,
with occasional flashes of type 1.

  {% include image.html url="97P-R6-1056.jpg"
     description="My character (right) cheering at a football match"
     attribution="Anna Didžbaniene" %}

At the time, exhaustion made it difficult to tell the difference between type
2 and type 3. Looking back, the uncertainty comes from somewhere else. I do
not particularly mind having fumbled parts of the game in front of other
players, but I do wonder whether my difficulty fully inhabiting the character
made the experience less enjoyable for them. If so, that would push many of
those moments into the type 3 category for me.

For example, I failed to follow through on several story lines involving other
characters, simply because I was stressed and not a particularly effective
vehicle for my character. In many emotional scenes, I struggled to portray the
character's emotions convincingly, if at all. During secondary-group meetings,
I often struggled to improvise and respond in a meaningful way and slipped into
the role of an observer instead. Taken together, this must have affected other
players' experience to some extent, disrupting story lines and breaking
immersion.

## The bleed
Over the course of the game, I gradually realized that my character would
either have to sacrifice other innocent characters in order to survive or die
with dignity. Despite my difficulty portraying the character, the gradual
realization during conversations with their child, followed by the decision,
the acceptance, and finally the violent act itself, became an unexpectedly
emotional sequence for me.

  {% include image.html url="97P-R6-491.jpg"
     description="My character after receiving a letter"
     attribution="Anna Didžbaniene" %}

In the LARP community, this is apparently referred to as _bleed_: the
spillover of emotions between player and character.

I am not ashamed, but still surprised that, while I was waiting in the off-game
room for the game to end after my character had just died, there were tears. In
my case, those emotions stayed with me for nearly a week after the game.

**UPDATE (2026-09-04):** A friend pointed out to me that some people experience
similar reactions to other forms of fiction as well, such as novels or video
games.

Personally, although I used to think of video games as the ultimate form of
interactive media, my experience with this LARP made me realize that, for me at
least, on a spectrum from acting to passive consumption, video games still fall
much closer to the latter, leaving a significant gap in terms of immersion and
meaningful interaction. The only time I have felt anything approaching bleed
from a video game was after the first chapter of _Danganronpa V3 (2017)_, which
affected me enough that I put the game down for a week.

# Would I do it again?
The people were lovely, the food was great, [the location was mesmerizing][9],
and even though I am still unsure whether I had "fun", it was certainly a
memorable experience. The bleed also suggests that the game made me care about
an imagined person, their child, and a decision they had to make strongly enough
that the emotions survived the character's death and followed me home for days.
Clearly, something in the medium has resonated quite powerfully with me.

Shortly after returning from the LARP, I also spent some time as a cook at [the
Fantasy LARP camp][6] in the Czech Republic, co-organized by my wife, so I
think I am becoming more comfortable in LARP-adjacent settings and am slowly
building some social ties with other players.

  {% include image.html url="97P-R6-245.jpg"
     description="The starting scene of my character (left) and other immigration officers"
     attribution="Anna Didžbaniene" %}

_Poets_ will not have another run any time soon, since the military hospital in
Terezín is now due to undergo repairs. Still, trying another, perhaps smaller,
LARP now feels like something I might be willing, and perhaps even excited, to
do, whether international or local.

Then again, despite reassurance from other players, the organizers, and my
wife, as well as my own realization that the LARP was more demanding than I had
anticipated, I still worry that I may be too disruptive a presence at future
LARPs, even smaller ones. I am not sure whether my lack of social and acting
skills, together with the distinct lack of type 1 fun, can be substantially
improved simply by attending more LARPs, potentially at other players' expense,
or whether there are things I should work on first outside the game. And if I
cannot improve enough, I still wonder whether it might be better to leave this
particular kind of fun to people who are better equipped for it and haven't
spent the last decade of their lives being as socially withdrawn as I have.
